Hydraulic hoses are pressurized lines that help machinery and heavy equipment perform work, sometimes under thousands of psi. They are the most common hose assemblies a branch builds. They are also the most dangerous to get wrong. This module covers hydraulic hose types, fluids and ratings, the thread and connection families, how fittings seal and why they leak, how to identify a fitting with calipers and gauges, fitting styles, and hydraulic safety. Module 9 covers building and testing assemblies.

### Learning objectives

After this module you will be able to:

1. Tell braided hose from spiral hose and match each to its pressure and impulse needs.
2. Read SAE and EN hose designations on a layline.
3. Check the hose's tube against the hydraulic fluid and the temperature.
4. Describe how NPT, NPSM, JIC, SAE 45°, ORFS, O-ring boss, BSP, metric, JIS, and flange connections seal.
5. Tell parallel threads from tapered threads, and name the three sealing methods and the common causes of leaks.
6. Identify a thread using calipers, a thread pitch gauge, and a seat angle gauge.
7. Describe fitting styles, elbows, orientation, and quick couplers.
8. Explain why hose and fittings should come from one maker's system, crimped to that maker's spec.
9. Explain the hydraulic injection hazard and how to avoid it.
10. Gather what the shop needs to build a replacement hydraulic assembly in P21.

> ⚠️ SAFETY & COMPLIANCE: Never hold, bend, or run a hand along a hydraulic hose under pressure. A pinhole leak can inject oil through skin, and the injury can look minor at first, but has life threatening potential.

## 7.1 Hydraulic Hose Types

Hydraulic hose has a nitrile or similar oil-resistant tube, steel wire reinforcement, and a tough cover. The reinforcement sets the pressure range.

| Construction | Typical designations | Pressure range | Where it fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 wire braid | SAE 100R1AT, EN 853 1SN | Medium | General hydraulics, return lines, lighter equipment |
| 2 wire braid | SAE 100R2AT, EN 853 2SN | High | Most mobile and plant hydraulics |
| Compact braid | EN 857 1SC and 2SC, SAE 100R16 and R17 | Medium to high, with a tighter bend radius | Tight routing on modern equipment |
| 4 and 6 wire spiral | SAE 100R12, R13, R15; EN 856 4SP and 4SH | Very high; R13 and R15 hold 5,000 and 6,000 psi in every size they cover | Heavy equipment main lines with pressure surges |
| Suction and return | SAE 100R4 | Low, with a wire helix for vacuum | Pump inlets and return lines |
| Thermoplastic | SAE 100R7 and R8 | Medium to high | Light weight; nonconductive versions for aerial lifts and utility trucks |

[IMAGE EXAMPLE: Cut ends of 1-wire braid, 2-wire braid, and 4-spiral hydraulic hose side by side, showing the wire layers]

### Braid or spiral?

- **Braid** is flexible and fits most jobs.
- **Spiral** handles very high pressure and repeated pressure surges (impulse) better. It is stiffer and needs a larger bend radius.
- Replace a hose with an equal or greater type. Check the pressure and impulse rating before you go from spiral to braid.

### Reading a hydraulic layline

A typical layline: "Manufacturer 100R2AT/2SN -8 1/2 in 4000 PSI 27.6 MPa 2Q25."

| Part | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Manufacturer | The maker and often its product line |
| 100R2AT/2SN | Meets SAE 100R2AT and EN 853 2SN |
| -8, 1/2 in | Dash size and ID |
| 4000 PSI, 27.6 MPa | Working pressure in psi and metric |
| 2Q25 | Date code: when the hose was made (the format varies by maker) |

### What wears out hydraulic hose

| Cause | What it looks like | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| Bending tighter than the minimum bend radius | Cracks or a kink at the bend; failure near the fitting | Longer hose, elbow fittings, better routing |
| Twisting during install | Layline spirals around the hose | Keep the layline straight when installing |
| Abrasion | Cover worn through to the wire | Sleeves, guards, clamps to stop rubbing |
| Heat | Hard, cracked cover and tube | Hose rated for the temperature; heat shields |
| Wrong fitting or bad crimp | Fitting blows off or leaks at the ferrule | Hose and fittings from one maker's system, crimped to that maker's spec (Module 9) |
| Too short | Hose pulled tight at full travel; failure at the fitting | Measure with the machine at full travel; hose shortens up to 4% under pressure |

> 📌 INDUSTRY INSIGHT: Use hose and fittings from one manufacturer's system, crimped to that manufacturer's spec. Mixing brands is a common cause of fittings blowing off.

> 💡 KEY TAKEAWAY: Read the layline before you quote a replacement. It gives you the maker, the hose type, the dash size, and the working pressure.

## 7.2 Fluids, Temperature, and Impulse

Hydraulic hose is rated for pressure, and also for the fluid, the temperature, and how often the pressure cycles. Check all four before you quote.

### Hydraulic fluids

| Fluid | Where you see it | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Petroleum hydraulic oil | Most mobile and plant hydraulics | Standard nitrile-tube hydraulic hose fits |
| Water-glycol | Fire-resistant systems: steel mills, die casting, mining | Most hydraulic hose handles it at a lower top temperature; check the maker's chart |
| Phosphate ester | Fire-resistant systems: aircraft ground equipment, turbines, some mills | Standard nitrile hose fails; use hose the maker rates for phosphate ester |
| Biodegradable (vegetable or synthetic ester) | Forestry, marine, farm and municipal equipment near water | Check the maker's chart for the fluid and temperature |
| Diesel and fuel | Fuel lines on equipment | Use hose rated for fuel |

When the customer is unsure of the fluid, ask for the fluid on the machine's reservoir label or the SDS.

### Temperature

Most standard hydraulic hose is rated from about −40°F to 212°F; some lines run to 250°F or higher. Check both the oil temperature and the temperature around the hose. Hose near an engine, exhaust, or furnace runs far hotter than the oil.

### Impulse

Impulse is the repeated rise and fall of pressure each time a valve moves. Hose makers test hose to a set number of pressure cycles. Spiral hose and the higher-rated braided hose are rated for more cycles. Machines with fast, repeating cycles (excavators, presses, log loaders) need hose with an impulse rating to match.

> ⚠️ SAFETY & COMPLIANCE: Match the hose to the highest pressure spike, the fluid, and the hottest temperature. A hose rated for the oil and pressure fails early when it runs over its temperature rating.

> 💡 KEY TAKEAWAY: On a hydraulic hose, run STAMPED like any other hose. Media means the exact hydraulic fluid; pressure means the highest spike.

## 7.3 Thread and Connection Types

Every hydraulic connection seals one of three ways: on the threads, on a metal seat, or on a soft seal (an O-ring or a bonded washer). Find how a fitting seals first; it narrows the search.

| Connection | Thread | Seals on | How to spot it |
|---|---|---|---|
| NPT and NPTF | Tapered, 60° thread form | The threads (with sealant, or a tight dryseal fit on NPTF) | Threads get smaller toward the end; no seat or O-ring |
| NPSM | Straight 60° pipe thread | The 30° nose of the female swivel seats on the 30° chamfer machined into the male: a metal-to-metal seal | Female swivel on pipe-thread hose ends; mates with an NPT or NPSM male |
| JIC 37° flare | Straight UN/UNF | Metal 37° cone seat | Male has a 37° cone at the tip; the most common hydraulic fitting in North America |
| SAE 45° flare | Straight UN/UNF | Metal 45° seat | Looks like JIC; used on low-pressure fuel, oil, and refrigerant lines |
| ORFS (O-ring face seal) | Straight UN | O-ring in a groove on the flat face of the male | Flat face with an O-ring; very leak resistant |
| SAE O-ring boss (ORB) | Straight UN/UNF, same threads as JIC | O-ring at the base of the male thread, against the port | O-ring sits behind the threads; used in pumps, valves, and cylinder ports |
| BSPP | Straight 55° Whitworth thread | Bonded washer, O-ring, or a 60° cone seat | British standard; common on European and Asian equipment |
| BSPT | Tapered 55° thread | The threads | Looks like NPT; the pitch matches in some sizes |
| Metric DIN 24° cone | Straight metric thread | 24° cone, often with an O-ring | Light (L) and heavy (S) series; sized by the tube OD it fits |
| JIS and Komatsu style | Metric or BSP thread | 30° seat | Japanese equipment |
| SAE Code 61 and Code 62 flange | No thread on the hose end; four bolts | O-ring in the flange face | Large, high-pressure lines; Code 62 is the higher pressure series |

[IMAGE EXAMPLE: Male ends of NPT, JIC 37°, ORFS, O-ring boss, BSPP, and metric 24° fittings lined up and labeled]

[IMAGE EXAMPLE: NPSM and NPT: an NPSM female swivel seals on a 30° seat, and the threads only hold]

> ⚠️ SAFETY & COMPLIANCE: Some different thread types screw together for a few turns, then leak or blow apart under pressure. JIC 37° and SAE 45° threads match in some sizes, and their seat angles differ. Never interchange them: the seal will not hold. NPT and BSPT look alike. Never force a fitting, and never use thread sealant to try and make a mismatched pair seal.

> 📌 INDUSTRY INSIGHT: Heavy equipment built in the US uses mostly JIC, ORFS, and O-ring boss. European and Asian machines often use BSPP, metric, or JIS connections. Ask who made the machine; it narrows the search fast.

## 7.4 How Fittings Seal

Most hydraulic leaks start with one wrong idea: that every threaded fitting seals on its threads. Many fittings seal on an O-ring, a metal cone, a cutting ring, or a bonded washer, and the threads only hold the parts together. Tightening harder won't stop a leak on these fittings. It strips threads, flattens the seat, or cuts the O-ring.

[IMAGE EXAMPLE: Common hydraulic adapters: JIC, ORB, NPT, and ORFS ends on elbows, tees, and straight fittings]

### Holding and sealing

Every fitting does two jobs:

1. **Hold** the connection together under pressure.
2. **Seal** the fluid in.

On some fittings the threads do both. On others the threads only supply the clamping force, and a separate surface seals. On an ORFS fitting, the threads pull the flat face tight against the O-ring and never touch the oil.

The table groups the common connection types under the three sealing methods.

| Sealing method | Connection type | Typical connections | What seals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soft seal | O-ring | ORB, ORFS | A rubber O-ring |
| Metal seat | Metal to metal | JIC 37°, SAE 45°, DIN 2353 cutting ring | A machined cone or a cutting ring |
| Threads | Tapered thread | NPT, NPTF, BSPT | Tapered threads wedged together |
| Soft seal or metal seat | Parallel thread | BSPP, metric | An O-ring, a bonded washer, or a seat |
| Soft seal | Bonded washer | BSPP and metric male ends | Rubber bonded to a steel washer |

A scratch, dent, or dirt on a sealing surface leaks even when the two fittings match. Check the seat, the face, and the O-ring before the fitting goes in.

### Parallel and tapered threads

[IMAGE EXAMPLE: BSPP and BSPT: a parallel thread keeps the same diameter, and a tapered thread gets smaller toward the end]

- **Parallel (straight) threads** keep the same diameter from end to end: BSPP, metric, ORB, JIC, and ORFS. The threads hold; an O-ring, bonded washer, or metal seat seals.
- **Tapered threads** get smaller toward the end: NPT, NPTF, and BSPT. Tightening wedges the threads together, and the threads are the seal.

| Feature | Parallel thread | Tapered thread |
|---|---|---|
| Thread shape | Same diameter end to end | Gets smaller toward the end |
| Do the threads seal? | No | Yes |
| Extra seal needed | O-ring, bonded washer, or seat | Thread sealant or PTFE tape on NPT |
| Reuse | Many times, with a new O-ring or washer | Limited; the threads deform each time |
| Overtightening | Less sensitive | More sensitive; can crack a port |

Mating the wrong thread forms leaks right away or ruins the threads.

### O-ring seals: ORB and ORFS

[IMAGE EXAMPLE: ORB and ORFS: the ORB O-ring sits behind the threads, and the ORFS O-ring sits in the flat face]

- **ORB (O-ring boss):** the O-ring sits at the base of the male thread and seals against the chamfer in the port.
- **ORFS (O-ring face seal):** the O-ring sits in a groove on the male's flat face and seals against the flat face of the female.

O-ring fittings resist leaks and vibration well and go back together many times with a new O-ring. Most new equipment uses them. Match the O-ring material to the fluid (Module 16).

### Metal-to-metal seals: JIC, SAE 45°, and DIN

[IMAGE EXAMPLE: JIC 37° and SAE 45° male flares side by side with their seat angles marked]

Two machined surfaces press together, with no rubber in the seal.

- **JIC 37°** and **SAE 45°** flares seal cone to seat. Their threads match in some sizes and they do not seal to each other. Never interchange them.
- **DIN 2353 cutting ring** fittings bite into a metal tube as the nut tightens. The ring grips the tube and seals it.

Metal seals handle high pressure and heat and last a long time. A nicked or flattened cone leaks, and more torque only flattens it further.

### Thread seals: NPT, NPTF, and BSPT

[IMAGE EXAMPLE: NPT and NPTF male threads side by side: NPT seals with sealant or tape, and NPTF is a dryseal thread]

The threads deform slightly as they tighten and form the seal.

- **NPT** needs thread sealant or PTFE tape. Keep tape off the first thread so pieces don't get into the system.
- **NPTF (dryseal)** threads are made to seal metal to metal without sealant.
- **BSPT** seals on the threads with sealant, like NPT, and has a 55° thread.

Thread seals are simple and easy to find. They have limited reuse and are sensitive to torque.

### Bonded washers

[IMAGE EXAMPLE: A BSPP male adapter with two bonded sealing washers]

A bonded washer (often called a Dowty washer) is a steel washer with a rubber ring bonded to its inside edge. It seals a BSPP or metric male end against a flat port face. It is low cost and easy to install. Replace it each time the fitting comes off.

### Comparing the methods

| Connection | Leak resistance | Reuse | Vibration | Installation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ORFS | Excellent | Excellent | Excellent | Easy |
| ORB | Excellent | Excellent | Excellent | Easy |
| BSPP with O-ring or bonded washer | Excellent | Excellent | Excellent | Easy |
| DIN 2353 cutting ring | Excellent | Good | Excellent | Moderate |
| JIC 37° | Very good | Very good | Good | Moderate |
| NPT | Good | Limited | Moderate | Difficult |

The machine and its pressure, heat, and vibration decide the connection. Replace like with like.

### Why fittings leak

[IMAGE EXAMPLE: Leaking NPT fitting troubleshooting: loose fit, no thread sealing, overtightening, and damaged threads]

Most leaks can be prevented. The common causes:

- BSP mixed with NPT
- A damaged or missing O-ring
- A scratched or flattened cone seat
- Cross-threading
- Too little or too much torque
- Dirt on a sealing surface
- A damaged ferrule or cutting ring used again
- Poorly machined fittings

Customers often blame the fitting. Installation errors cause a large share of field failures.

### Installing fittings

[IMAGE EXAMPLE: Confirm thread type, sealing method, and torque: don't force a fitting, don't mix standards, seal tapered threads, and use the right torque]

Pass this checklist along to customers who install their own fittings:

1. Confirm the fitting standard on both sides.
2. Inspect the sealing surfaces.
3. Replace damaged O-rings and bonded washers.
4. Lubricate the O-ring with clean system fluid when the maker calls for it.
5. Tighten to the maker's torque with a calibrated torque tool.
6. Keep sealant and tape off O-ring and flare fittings.
7. Never overtighten.

> ⚠️ SAFETY & COMPLIANCE: Sealant or tape on an O-ring or flare fitting doesn't help it seal, and loose pieces can get into the hydraulic system. Use sealant on tapered pipe threads only.

> 💡 KEY TAKEAWAY: Find how a fitting seals before you match it. Threads hold; the seal may be an O-ring, a cone, a ring, a washer, or the threads themselves.

## 7.5 Identifying a Thread

You need three tools: **calipers**, a **thread pitch gauge** (inch and metric), and a **seat angle gauge**. Work through the steps in order.

### Steps

1. **Find the seal.** Look for an O-ring on the face, an O-ring behind the threads, a cone seat, a bonded washer, or only threads (7.3 and 7.4).
2. **Tapered or straight?** Hold the fitting at eye level. Tapered threads get smaller toward the end; measure the first and last full thread with calipers to check.
3. **Measure the thread diameter.** On a male thread, measure the outside diameter across the threads. On a female thread, measure the inside diameter. A female measures a little smaller than its matching male.
4. **Measure the pitch.** Try the inch leaves first; find the one that sits flat in the threads with no light showing. When no inch leaf fits, try metric.
5. **Check the seat angle.** Set the seat gauge against the cone or flare: JIC 37°, SAE flare 45°, DIN metric 24°, JIS and NPSM 30°. A BSPP 60° cone reads 30° on the seat gauge, the same as JIS. The thread tells them apart.
6. **Match it to a chart.** Use the tables below and the maker's thread ID guide. Never identify a fitting by thread size alone; several standards share sizes.

[IMAGE EXAMPLE: Calipers measuring a male thread OD, a pitch gauge seated in the threads, and a seat angle gauge on a JIC cone]

| -5 | 1/2-20 |
| -6 | 9/16-18 |
| -8 | 3/4-16 |
| -10 | 7/8-14 |
| -12 | 1-1/16-12 |
| -16 | 1-5/16-12 |
| -20 | 1-5/8-12 |
| -24 | 1-7/8-12 |

### NPT and BSP pipe threads

Pipe thread sizes are nominal. The actual outside diameter is larger than the size.

| Size (in) | NPT threads per inch | NPT male OD (in) | BSP threads per inch | BSP male OD (in) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1/8 | 27 | 0.405 | 28 | 0.383 |
| 1/4 | 18 | 0.540 | 19 | 0.518 |
| 3/8 | 18 | 0.675 | 19 | 0.656 |
| 1/2 | 14 | 0.840 | 14 | 0.825 |
| 3/4 | 14 | 1.050 | 14 | 1.041 |
| 1 | 11-1/2 | 1.315 | 11 | 1.309 |

[IMAGE EXAMPLE: Measuring an NPT thread: OD with calipers, threads per inch with a pitch gauge, the taper at both ends, and a final check with an NPT ring gauge]

> 💡 KEY TAKEAWAY: A 1/2 in pipe thread measures about 0.84 in across. When a customer says "half-inch fitting," it's best to measure before you assume.

> 📌 INDUSTRY INSIGHT: At 1/2 in and 3/4 in, NPT and BSP both have 14 threads per inch, so the pitch gauge can't tell them apart. Measure the OD (0.840 in NPT, 0.825 in BSP), check for taper, look for a seat or washer, and ask where the machine was built.

## 7.6 Fitting Styles, Quick Couplers, and Crimping Basics

### Fitting styles

| Style | What it is | Ask the customer |
|---|---|---|
| Male (fixed) | Male thread fixed to the stem | Thread type and size |
| Female swivel | Female nut that spins on the stem | Thread type and size |
| 45° and 90° elbows | Bent stems; short or long drop | Angle, drop length, and orientation to the other end |
| Bent tube | A longer formed tube for tight spots | Bend angle and tube length |
| Banjo | A ring fitting with a hollow bolt | Bolt thread and ring size |
| Flange head | Code 61 or Code 62 head with a split or captive flange | Code, flange size, and bolt pattern |

With an elbow on each end, measure the **orientation**: sight down the hose with the far elbow pointing straight up, and read the angle clockwise to the near elbow. Write it in the order line's extended description.

### Quick couplers

| Coupler | Where it is used | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Poppet (ag style, ISO 7241-1 A) | Tractor remotes and implements | Body size and thread; most tractors take 1/2 in body |
| Flat face (ISO 16028) | Skid steers, attachments, construction equipment | Body size; flat face keeps dirt out and oil in |
| Threaded-to-connect | High-pressure tools, hydraulic jacks, demolition tools | Pressure rating; connects under residual pressure |

Match the coupler to the machine's coupler style and body size. Two styles of the same nominal size don't connect.

### Crimping basics

The shop builds the assembly (Module 9). You need to know enough to give the shop what it needs and to answer the customer.

- **One system.** Hose, fittings, and dies come from one maker's system. The maker's crimp chart gives the die and the crimp diameter for each hose and fitting.
- **Crimp diameter.** The shop measures the finished crimp with calipers and checks it against the chart's tolerance.
- **Insertion depth.** Mark the insertion depth, then push the hose in to the mark.
- **Skive or no-skive.** Some fittings require removing the cover at the hose end (skiving) before crimping. Most current fittings are no-skive. Check the crimp chart if you're unsure.

> ⚠️ SAFETY & COMPLIANCE: A crimp outside the maker's tolerance can blow off under pressure. Never re-crimp a used fitting. Never build a hydraulic assembly from another maker's fittings. When the fitting is out of stock, bring in your Manager.

## 7.7 Hydraulic Safety, Industry Applications, and P21

### Safety

| Hazard | What happens | What to tell the customer |
|---|---|---|
| Fluid injection | A pinhole leak shoots oil through skin. It can look like a small cut and needs emergency medical care. | Never feel for a leak. Hold cardboard or wood at arm's length to find the leak. Then shut down and relieve pressure before touching the hose. |
| Stored pressure | A system can hold pressure after the engine stops; a raised boom holds pressure in the cylinder. | Lower the load to the ground and relieve pressure before removing any hose. |
| Hose whip | A hose or fitting that lets go under pressure whips with great force. | Replace damaged hose. Use hose restraints where a failure could cause an injury. |
| Hot oil | Hydraulic oil can be hot enough to burn. | Let the system cool before work. |

> ⚠️ SAFETY & COMPLIANCE: Never repair a hydraulic hose with tape, a clamp, or a splice. Replace damaged hose.

### Industry applications

| Industry | Equipment | Typical hose and connections |
|---|---|---|
| Heavy equipment | Excavators, loaders, dozers | Spiral hose on main lines, 2-wire braid on smaller circuits; JIC, ORFS, Code 61 and 62 flanges |
| Agriculture | Tractors, combines, sprayers, implements | 2-wire and compact braid; JIC, ORB, and ag quick couplers |
| Pulp and paper | Presses, roll handling, chip and log equipment | 2-wire braid and spiral; JIC, ORFS, and flanges |
| Chemical | Valve actuators, hydraulic power units | 1- and 2-wire braid; often stainless fittings for corrosion |

[IMAGE EXAMPLE: An excavator with its hydraulic hoses and components labeled]

### What to gather for a replacement hydraulic assembly

1. Machine make, model, and the hose's location on the machine.
2. The layline: hose type, dash size, working pressure.
3. Both ends: fitting style, thread type, size, and seat.
4. Overall length and, with two elbows, the orientation.
5. Fluid, temperature, and any guard or sleeve.
6. When the customer needs it. Most often, the answer is "now".

### In P21

1. Press **F8** and check Customer Sales History. Many customers reorder the same assembly; the kit item may already be on file.
2. Press **F2** and check net available on the bulk hose and both fittings from one maker's system.
3. Enter the assembly as a **kit item** (Module 9) with the spec in the line's **extended description** (Module 6).
4. Check the shop's schedule before you promise a date and time.

> 💡 KEY TAKEAWAY: When an equipment customer calls with a burst hose, get the machine make and model, the layline, and both fitting ends. The shop can build it with this info.

## 7.8 Case Study, What If, and Rookie Mistakes

### Case study: the boom hose

A contractor brings a burst hose to the counter: "Excavator boom hose. The crew is standing around. How fast can you make one?"

1. **Safety first.** The rep asks what it carried: hydraulic oil. The rep puts on gloves, then handles the hose.
2. **Layline.** EN 856 4SH, -16, 5,500 psi working pressure.
3. **Ends.** One end is a flange head; the seal is an O-ring on the face, four bolts. The flange head diameter, measured with calipers, matches Code 62. The other end is a female swivel with a flat face and O-ring groove on the mating male: ORFS, 1-7/16-12 thread, -16.
4. **Length and orientation.** 64 in overall. The flange head and the 90° ORFS elbow sit at 90° from each other.
5. **Failure.** The cover is worn through to the wire where the hose rubbed the boom. The rep asks for a sleeve on the new hose.
6. **P21.** F8 shows no earlier order for this assembly. F2 shows the 4SH hose, the Code 62 flange head, and the -16 ORFS elbow in one maker's system, all in stock.
7. **Order.** The rep enters the kit item with the layline, ends, length, orientation, and sleeve in the extended description, and checks the shop: ready in 45 minutes.

The abrasion caused the failure. The sleeve keeps the new hose from failing the same way.

### What if...

| Situation | What to do | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| The layline is worn off | Measure ID, identify both ends, ask the machine make and model and the system pressure | A guessed rating can burst |
| A fitting threads on and then binds | Stop and identify both threads | Mismatched threads leak or blow apart |
| The customer wants a cheaper braided hose in place of spiral | Check pressure and impulse ratings; bring in your Manager | Spiral handles surges braid may not |
| The machine runs a fire-resistant fluid | Get the exact fluid and check the maker's chart | Phosphate ester attacks standard hose |
| The customer wants fittings from another brand on your hose | Use one maker's system | Mixed systems blow off |
| The hose sits next to an exhaust | Add a heat sleeve and check the temperature rating | Heat cracks the cover and tube |
| The customer asks to tape a leak until the new hose is ready | Tell them to shut it down and relieve pressure | Tape often hides an injection hazard |

### Rookie mistakes

| Mistake | What it causes | The habit that prevents it |
|---|---|---|
| Guessing a thread by eye | A fitting that leaks or blows off | Use calipers, a pitch gauge, and a seat gauge |
| Calling a 1/2 in pipe thread 0.5 in | The wrong fitting ordered | Measure the OD: 0.84 in |
| Mixing JIC 37° and SAE 45° | A leak at the seat | Check the seat angle |
| Stepping down from spiral to braid | Early failure from surges | Match pressure and impulse ratings |
| Skipping the orientation on two elbows | An assembly that won't line up | Measure and record orientation |
| Quoting without the fluid | A tube that swells on special fluids | Ask the exact fluid |
| Feeling for a leak | Fluid injection | Hold cardboard or wood at arm's length to find the leak. Then shut down and relieve pressure before touching the hose. |

## 7.9 Hydraulic Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Braid / spiral | Wire reinforcement woven in a braid / wound in layers |
| SAE 100R series | SAE J517 hydraulic hose types (100R1, 100R2, 100R12, and more) |
| 1SN, 2SN, 4SP, 4SH | EN hydraulic hose types |
| Impulse | Repeated pressure cycles each time a valve moves |
| Phosphate ester | A fire-resistant fluid that needs special hose |
| NPT / NPTF | Tapered pipe thread / dry-seal tapered pipe thread |
| JIC 37° | A flare fitting that seals on a 37° metal cone |
| SAE 45° | A flare fitting that seals on a 45° seat |
| ORFS | O-ring face seal |
| ORB | SAE O-ring boss: an O-ring behind the male thread |
| NPSM | Straight pipe thread; the female swivel seals on a 30° seat |
| Parallel / tapered thread | Same diameter end to end / gets smaller toward the end |
| Bonded washer | A steel washer with a bonded rubber seal; also called a Dowty washer |
| DIN 2353 | Metric cutting ring tube fitting |
| BSPP / BSPT | British parallel / tapered pipe thread |
| DIN 24° | A metric fitting that seals on a 24° cone |
| Code 61 / Code 62 | SAE four-bolt flange series; 62 is the higher pressure |
| Pitch | Threads per inch, or millimeters between threads |
| Seat angle | The angle of the sealing cone or flare |
| Female swivel | A female nut that spins on the fitting stem |
| Drop | The length of an elbow's bend |
| Orientation | The angle between two elbows on one hose |
| Crimp diameter | The measured diameter of a finished crimp |
| Skive | Removing the cover at the hose end before crimping |
| Flat face coupler | A quick coupler with flat mating faces (ISO 16028) |
| Fluid injection | Oil forced through skin by a pinhole leak |
